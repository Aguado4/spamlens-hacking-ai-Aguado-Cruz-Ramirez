From your message to a verdict in three passes

  1. 01

    Split into pieces it knows

    A SentencePiece tokenizer cuts the message into subwords from a 250,000-token vocabulary shared across about 100 languages, so slang like "txt" or "ur" and a Spanish "ganaste" all get a representation.

  2. 02

    Scored by a compact transformer

    Twelve attention layers of Goodmotion/spam-mail-classifier, a Multilingual MiniLM, read the whole message at once. We fine-tuned it on SMS because it was originally trained on email subject lines.

  3. 03

    Explained, not just labeled

    We remove each word in turn and measure how much the spam probability moves. Words that raise it are marked in red, words that lower it in green, so you can judge the verdict yourself.

The published model was trained on emails. Text messages needed a second lesson.

Taken as published, the pretrained model flagged most ordinary text messages as spam: informal SMS full of abbreviations and numbers looked suspicious to a model raised on email subjects. Fine-tuning the same network on our training split fixed that.

We also measured it against a classic word-frequency model, and we show that result too: on English SMS the two are practically tied. The transformer ranks messages more reliably and reads beyond the English vocabulary it was trained on.

Your message is read once, then forgotten

SpamLens has no database. The text you paste lives in memory only while the model scores it, and it is never logged, stored or used for training.

We took the same care with the data we learned from. The statistics behind our analysis can be published under ε-differential privacy with the Laplace mechanism from Dwork and Roth's The Algorithmic Foundations of Differential Privacy: calibrated noise that keeps every chart readable while making it impossible to tell whether any single message was in the data.

Questions worth asking

Can I trust the verdict completely?

No classifier is perfect. On our test set it still let a few spam messages through and raised a handful of false alarms. Treat a "spam" verdict as a strong warning, and never act on a message that asks for codes, passwords or payments, whatever the verdict.

Why was my message flagged?

Look at the red words in the result. The model learned that prizes, short codes, prices, links and pressure to act now are typical of spam. A legitimate message that forwards a promotion or includes a phone number can trip the same cues.

Does it work in Spanish?

Partly. The underlying model was trained on many languages, but our fine-tuning data is English SMS from the UK, and that narrowed it: in our checks it still caught typical Spanish prize and bank scams, while it missed a French one it used to catch. Treat non-English verdicts as a hint, not a ruling.

What counts as spam here?

Unsolicited bulk messages: prize and lottery scams, premium-rate traps, marketing you did not ask for and phishing. Messages from people and services you deal with count as legitimate.

Can I call it from code?

Yes. Send a POST to /api/classify/ with a JSON body like {"message": "..."} and you get the probability, the verdict and the word weights back as JSON.
